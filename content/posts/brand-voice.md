Every company I've worked with has had a brand voice guide. Very few of them had a brand voice. The guide was a PDF someone made during a rebrand, full of words like "approachable" and "bold," and nobody had opened it since the launch party.

That works when one person writes everything. It falls apart the minute you have a team. I lead 23 people across the U.S., Mexico, Latin America, the U.K., Europe and Ukraine. They write in different time zones, for different practices, sometimes in different languages. If the voice lives only in my head, it isn't a voice. It's a bottleneck.

## Adjectives aren't instructions

"Warm but confident" sounds great in a workshop. Hand it to a writer at 4 p.m. on a deadline, and it means nothing. Warm how? Confident compared with what?

The fix is to stop describing the voice and start showing it. For every trait, I want three things in the guide:

- What it means in plain terms ("We say what we think, then back it up.")
- A before-and-after rewrite of a real sentence
- What it does *not* mean ("Confident isn't loud, and it isn't promising results we can't guarantee.")

That last one does more work than people expect. Most voice problems come from writers overshooting the target, not missing it. A writer told to be "bold" will often swing into hype. A writer told to be "friendly" will add exclamation points until the page sounds like a birthday card. Naming the failure mode heads that off before it starts.

## Build the guide around real copy

The most useful voice guides I've seen are mostly examples. Rules matter, but people learn voice by pattern, the same way they pick up an accent. So I fill the guide with real sentences from our own work: a strong homepage line, a good email subject, a social post that landed, an ad that didn't and why.

I also include a short glossary. Every brand has words it uses on purpose and words it avoids. In aesthetics, for example, the words you choose around results, recovery and risk carry real weight. A glossary makes those choices visible, so a new writer doesn't have to learn them through three rounds of edits.

## Write the rules for the hard moments

Nobody needs help sounding friendly in a welcome email. Voice breaks down in the awkward places: a price increase, an apology, a regulated claim, a complaint on social media. That's where the guide should spend its time.

One page on "how we say no" beats five pages on our personality. The same goes for how we handle bad news, how we talk about competitors and how we respond when a customer is upset. Those moments test a brand far more than a tagline does, and they're often written fast, by whoever is on shift.

## Make the edit teach

A guide gets people 70% of the way. Editing gets them the rest. When I edit someone's draft, I try to leave a reason along with the fix. "Cut this, it's hedging" is a note a writer can reuse next week. A silent rewrite teaches them nothing except that I'll fix it anyway.

Over time, the same notes show up less often. That's how you know the voice is spreading. When a writer starts catching their own hedges before I see the draft, the guide has done its job.

## Spread the ownership

On a large team, one editor can't review everything and shouldn't try. I'd rather build a few strong voice owners across regions and teams. They know the guide well, edit with the same standards and flag patterns back to me.

That also makes the voice more resilient. If one person leaves or goes on vacation, the brand doesn't drift for two weeks.

## Let it evolve

A voice that never changes is a voice nobody uses. When the team keeps breaking a rule for good reasons, the rule is probably wrong. Updating the guide beats winning the argument.

I review ours on a regular schedule and after any big launch. If a new channel or market needs a slightly different register, the guide should say so instead of leaving each writer to guess.

## Onboard writers into the voice

New writers shouldn't learn the voice by trial and error. I give every new hire the guide, a handful of strong examples and a short practice assignment in their first week. We edit it together, and I explain each note in terms of the guide.

That first session sets the tone. Writers learn that the voice has reasons behind it, and they learn how I give feedback before the stakes are high. Most people pick it up quickly once they see a few real before-and-after edits.

## The short version

Show, don't describe. Fill the guide with real examples, and spend your words on the hard moments. Edit in a way that teaches, share ownership of the voice and treat the guide like a living document, because the second it stops changing, people stop reading it.
