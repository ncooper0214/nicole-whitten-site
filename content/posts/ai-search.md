More and more people get answers from AI tools instead of clicking through a list of links. That's making plenty of marketers nervous and keeping consultants busy selling new acronyms.

Some things are changing. Fewer than you'd think. Here's how I'm approaching it.

## The basics matter more now

AI tools pull from content that's clear, well organized and trustworthy. That's what good search engine optimization content was supposed to be all along. If your content was written for humans first, you're in a better spot than you might think.

If it was keyword-stuffed filler, AI search is going to be rough.

The shift rewards companies that invested in quality and punishes the ones that chased volume. Thin pages written only to rank were always fragile. Now they're also easy for an AI tool to ignore.

![A line drawing of a chat bubble containing a small page with neat lines of text.](/img/chat.svg)

## Answer the question directly

AI tools look for content that answers a question cleanly. So I write that way:

- Put the direct answer near the top, then explain it.
- Use headings that sound like the questions people actually ask.
- Keep each section focused on one thing.
- Define terms plainly instead of assuming the reader knows them.

This also happens to be better for human readers. Funny how that works.

Clear structure helps too. Short sections, descriptive headings and simple lists make it easier for both people and tools to find the exact answer they need without wading through an essay first.

> “If your content was written for humans first, *you're in a better spot than you think.*”

## Be the source

AI tools are already very good at summarizing what's out there. If your content is another summary, there's no reason to cite you. Original data, real examples, a clear point of view and expertise you can prove are what make your content worth quoting.

It's the same thing that made our long-form content work at Nielsen. We had data nobody else had.

Every company has some version of this. It might be customer survey results, patterns from hundreds of projects or a strong opinion backed by experience. Find it, publish it and attach a real expert's name to it.

## Your brand has to be consistent everywhere

AI tools build a picture of your brand from everything they can find, not just your website. If your messaging is different on every channel, that picture gets muddy. Consistent positioning helps.

That includes the places you don't control directly: reviews, directories, partner pages and press coverage. Keeping your core description, services and proof points consistent across all of them makes it easier for any tool to describe you accurately.

![A line drawing of a central dot connected by flowing lines to several small pages.](/img/network.svg)

## Keep it current

Outdated content is a bigger risk when an AI tool might quote it as fact. Old pricing, retired services or stale statistics can end up in an answer about your company long after you've moved on.

A regular content audit is one of the simplest things you can do for AI search. Update what's still useful, and remove what isn't.

## Don't chase every new tactic

There's plenty of noise right now. I'd rather do the fundamentals well than jump on every new trick that might stop working next quarter.

That doesn't mean ignoring what's changing. I keep an eye on how our brand shows up in AI answers and adjust when something's off. But the fixes almost always come back to clearer content, better proof and more consistent messaging.

## Measure differently

Traditional search metrics don't tell the whole story anymore. If an AI tool answers a question using your content, the reader may never click through. Traffic can drop even when your visibility goes up.

So I look at a broader set of signals: branded search, direct traffic, mentions in AI answers, the quality of inbound leads and what prospects say when asked how they found us. None of these are perfect alone. Together, they give a much clearer picture than clicks.

It also helps to set expectations with leadership early. If the team knows traffic patterns may shift, a drop in clicks won't trigger a panic. The conversation can stay focused on whether the right people are finding the company and taking the next step.

The companies that handle this shift well will be the ones that keep investing in useful content, even when the old metrics get noisier. Readers and AI tools are both looking for the same thing: a trustworthy answer from someone who knows the subject.

## The short version

Write clearly for humans, and answer questions directly. Publish things only you can say, keep your messaging consistent everywhere and keep your content current. That's most of AI search, and it's most of good copy too.
