Every copywriter has watched a strong line get edited into mush. It happens in the review meeting, one reasonable-sounding note at a time. Could we add this? Can we soften that? What if we said "solutions"?

At a senior level, writing the copy is only half the job. The other half is getting it approved without watering it down.

## Agree on strategy first

If the first time executives see your thinking is the final draft, you're going to have a fight. I get agreement on the audience, the goal and the main message before anyone sees a headline.

Then the review becomes a question of whether the copy does what we already agreed it should, not a debate about taste.

A one-page creative brief does most of this work. It names the reader, the problem, the one thing we want them to believe and the proof we'll use. When leadership signs off on that page, the copy has something to stand on.

## Explain the why

"I like it" doesn't hold up against a CEO's opinion. "Our buyers told us this is their top concern, so we lead with it" does. I come to reviews ready to explain every big choice in terms of the reader and the business.

That doesn't mean defending every comma. Pick the hills that matter.

I decide those hills before the meeting. Usually it's the headline, the core message and anything that affects trust or accuracy. Smaller wording preferences are often worth giving up, especially if it buys goodwill for the parts that matter.

> “Pick the hills that matter. *Let the rest go.*”

## Listen for the worry behind the note

When an executive asks to add something, there's usually a real concern underneath. Maybe they're worried about a competitor, a big customer or a legal risk. If I can find the worry, I can often solve it without wrecking the copy.

The fix might be a different page or a sales asset. And when they're right, I change it.

Asking a simple question helps: "What are you worried a reader will think?" The answer often points to a better solution than the edit they suggested.

## Control the review process

Copy reviews go sideways when too many people weigh in at once. I try to keep the group small, share drafts with clear questions and set a deadline for feedback.

I also consolidate notes before making changes. When feedback conflicts, I bring the options back to the decision-maker instead of trying to satisfy everyone. Copy written to please a committee rarely pleases a reader.

## Show, don't argue

When there's a real disagreement, I'd rather show than debate. Two versions side by side. A quick test. Data from a similar page. Evidence moves people more than opinions do, including mine.

Testing doesn't have to be elaborate. A simple A/B test on an email subject line or landing page headline can settle a debate faster than another meeting.

## Build trust between projects

The best way to win the next review is to have delivered results on the last one. When leadership has seen your copy move numbers, they give you more room. That's why I track results and why I share them.

Trust also comes from consistency. When executives know you'll listen, explain your thinking and change course when you're wrong, they're more willing to back you when you hold firm.

## Bring the reader into the room

The most persuasive voice in a copy review usually isn't mine. It's the customer's. When I can, I bring real customer language into the discussion: quotes from interviews, sales call notes, reviews and support tickets.

It's much harder to argue with a customer's own words. If buyers keep describing a problem a certain way, that's a strong reason to use their language on the page, even if it isn't how the company would describe it internally.

This shifts the review from "What do we want to say?" to "What does the reader need to hear?" That's the question that leads to better copy, and it gives executives a way to change their minds without feeling like they lost an argument.

Customer language also makes copy stronger on its own. Readers recognize their own words. When a headline describes a problem the way they'd describe it, it feels written for them, and that's exactly the feeling good copy should create.

## The short version

Agree on strategy before showing copy, and explain choices in terms of the reader. Find the worry behind the note, keep the review group small and use evidence over opinion. Earn trust with results, so each review is easier than the last.
