The first thing that happens when a team gets AI writing tools is that everyone starts using them differently. One writer has a great prompt. Another pastes in the whole brief and hopes. A third doesn't use the tools at all.

Within a few weeks, the brand starts sounding like 10 different companies. Guardrails fix that.

## Put the brand voice where the AI can use it

Your voice guide probably lives in a PDF. AI can't follow a PDF it never sees. So the first step is turning the guide into something the tools actually use: shared instructions, example rewrites and a short list of words and claims to avoid.

This is where I spend much of my time at Adoreal. I build custom AI skills and automations for my team, so the voice and the rules are built in from the start instead of depending on whoever is writing.

A good setup includes more than tone. It covers the audience, approved messaging, product facts, formatting preferences and examples of strong copy. The more of that context lives in the tool, the less each writer has to remember.

## Decide what AI is allowed to do

Not every task should get the same treatment. I sort them into three groups:

- **AI drafts, human edits:** High-volume copy like social media variations and first-pass descriptions.
- **Human writes, AI assists:** Headlines, landing pages and campaigns, where AI helps with options and checks.
- **Human only:** Positioning, sensitive topics, regulated claims and anything that speaks for leadership.

Write it down. Ambiguity is how things slip through.

Share it widely too. Everyone who touches content, including freelancers and partners, should know which group a task falls into. When the rules live in one person's head, they get applied inconsistently.

> “AI can't follow a voice guide *it never sees.*”

## Build a banned list

Every brand has words and claims it should never use. In aesthetics, that includes promises about results and language that could mislead a patient. Other industries have their own versions.

I keep a short, specific list of what's off-limits and build it into our tools and review checklists. AI is good at checking a draft against a list like that. It's much less reliable at knowing the list exists unless you tell it.

## Always keep a human sign-off

Nothing goes out without a person reading it. The tools aren't the problem. The sign-off is how you catch the confident mistakes, the off-brand phrasing and the claims that sound fine but aren't.

In regulated industries like aesthetics, this isn't optional.

The reviewer should also own the result. If something goes wrong, "the AI wrote it" is never an acceptable answer. Clear ownership keeps people reading carefully.

## Audit what gets published

Every so often, pull a sample of what's been published and read it like an outsider. Does it sound like us? Is anything drifting? This is how you catch problems before a customer does.

I look for patterns as much as mistakes. If the same phrase keeps showing up across writers, the tools are probably producing it. That's a sign to update the instructions or add an example.

## Update it as you go

AI tools change fast. So do the ways your team uses them. Treat the guardrails like the voice guide: a living document you revisit, not a rulebook you write once.

I collect feedback from the team on what's working and what's frustrating. Writers usually spot gaps before anyone else does, and their fixes are often simple.

## Train the team on the why

Rules work best when people understand the reasons behind them. A writer who knows why a claim is banned will catch similar problems the rules don't cover. A writer who only knows the list will miss them.

So I don't just hand out the guardrails. I walk the team through them, explain the risks and share examples of what can go wrong. In aesthetics, that includes how small wording choices can affect patient trust and compliance.

Training also builds confidence. Writers who understand the boundaries use AI more effectively, because they're not second-guessing every output. They know where the tool helps, where it doesn't and when to bring a question to an editor.

I'd rather have a team that understands the reasoning and occasionally asks a question than a team that follows a list blindly and misses the problems the list never anticipated.

## The short version

Turn your voice guide into something AI can use. Decide which tasks get which level of human involvement, keep a banned list and make sure every piece has a human owner. Then audit the output regularly and keep improving the setup, so the brand still sounds like itself.
