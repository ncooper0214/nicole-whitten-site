Copy has a reputation problem with finance. To a CFO, it can look like a cost center full of people arguing about commas. If you want budget, headcount or a seat at the table, you need to show what the words are worth.

I've been able to do that at every stop, and never because I had perfect attribution. It's because I picked the right numbers and tracked them on purpose.

## Tie copy to a business outcome before you write

The mistake is trying to measure impact after the fact. By then, everything's tangled together. Instead, decide up front what the copy is supposed to change and how you'll know.

- Proposal copy should move close rates. At XPO, ours rose by as much as 22%.
- Long-form content should move organic traffic and pipeline. At Nielsen, our traffic grew 30% year over year.
- Web content should move engagement and action. At Meta, a global content initiative I led lifted engagement 35%.

Setting the metric early also changes how the work gets done. When a team knows the goal is close rates, they write differently than when the goal is "make it sound better."

## Count hours too

Not every win shows up in revenue. Many show up in hours. At Adoreal, we cut a site search engine optimization audit from about four hours to 15 minutes. At XPO, moving to better tools improved team efficiency by about 20%.

Finance understands hours. Turn them into dollars, and they'll understand you too.

The math doesn't need to be fancy. Multiply the hours saved by a reasonable hourly cost and by how often the task happens. A process that saves a few hours a week across a team adds up to real money over a year.

> “Finance understands hours. *Turn them into dollars,* and they'll understand you too.”

## Speak their language

CFOs don't care about engagement for its own sake. They care about revenue, cost, risk and efficiency. So frame your results that way. "Our new proposal process helped us win more business with the same headcount" lands better than "Our proposals are higher quality now."

Keep the story short. One page with three or four clear numbers beats a 30-slide deck. If finance wants more detail, they'll ask.

## Show the cost of bad copy too

Unclear pages create support tickets. Confusing proposals lose deals. Off-brand or noncompliant copy creates real risk. Sometimes the strongest case for copy is what it prevented.

When I can, I put a number on that too: support tickets tied to a confusing page, deals lost on a weak proposal or hours spent fixing content after launch. Those costs are real, even if they rarely show up in a marketing report.

## Build the case over time

One strong result can win a meeting. A steady record wins a budget. I keep a simple running log of what copy and content delivered: the metric, the before and after and the project behind it.

When budget season comes, I'm not scrambling to remember what worked. The case is already written.

## Report before you need something

If the only time finance hears from you is during budget season, you're already behind. A short, regular summary of what copy and content delivered builds trust long before you need it.

It also gives finance a chance to ask questions early. When they understand how you measure success, they're more likely to support the next request.

## Start with one project

If your team has never measured the return on copy, don't try to measure everything at once. Pick one project with a clear goal and a clean before-and-after, such as a landing page rewrite, a proposal template or an email sequence.

Measure it carefully, and share the result. One credible win makes the next conversation with finance much easier. It also teaches your team how to set goals and track them, so measurement becomes part of how the work gets done.

From there, expand gradually. A few well-measured projects build more trust than a dashboard full of numbers nobody fully believes.

It also helps to involve finance early. Ask them which numbers they trust and how they'd like to see results reported. When you measure what they already care about, in a format they recognize, your results land with far less debate.

## The short version

Set the metric before you write, and count hours as well as revenue. Translate results into the language finance uses, and show what bad copy costs. Keep a running record, and report consistently, so your case is already made when you ask for more.
