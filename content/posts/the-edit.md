I have a master's degree in creative nonfiction, and the most useful thing it taught me wasn't about writing. It was about editing. A first draft is raw material, and the real writing happens after.

Most people skip that part or rush it. They write, give it a quick read, fix the typos and ship. That's proofreading. Editing is something else.

## I edit in passes

Trying to fix everything in one read is how you miss things. I go through a draft several times, looking for one kind of problem each time:

- **Structure:** Does it start in the right place? Is the order doing any work?
- **Argument:** Is there one clear point, and does every section support it?
- **Paragraphs:** Does each one earn its spot, or is it repeating the last one?
- **Sentences:** Is anything hedged, padded or passive for no reason?
- **Words:** Is there a plainer word that means the same thing?

Structure first, words last. There's no point polishing a sentence you're about to cut.

## Give it some distance

The worst time to edit is right after you finish writing. You still remember what you meant, so you read what you intended instead of what's on the page.

Even a short break helps. I'll often step away for an hour, or overnight when the deadline allows. When I come back, the gaps and repeats jump out. If there's no time for a break, I change the format instead. Reading a draft on my phone or in a different font tricks my brain into seeing it fresh.

## The usual suspects

After years of editing, I can tell you most drafts have the same problems. Throat-clearing intros. "In order to" instead of "to." Three adjectives where one would do. A great sentence buried in the fourth paragraph that should be the opening line.

That last one shows up constantly. Writers often find their point halfway through the draft. The edit is where you move it to the top.

Other repeat offenders include sentences that start with "There is" or "It is," stacked qualifiers like "very" and "really," and vague verbs like "leverage" and "utilize" where "use" would do. None of these are crimes. Together, they make copy feel heavy.

## Read it out loud

It feels silly, but it works. Your ear catches rhythm problems, run-ons and clunky phrasing your eyes skip right over. If I run out of breath, the sentence is too long. If I stumble, so will the reader.

Reading aloud also exposes repetition. When the same word shows up three times in a paragraph, or every sentence starts the same way, you hear it immediately.

## Check the facts last, but always check them

Once the words are right, I go back for accuracy. Numbers, names, dates, product details and claims all get a second look. A great sentence with a wrong number is worse than a plain sentence with the right one, because it's memorable for the wrong reason.

In regulated industries, this step matters even more. A small factual slip can turn into a compliance problem.

## Editing other people is a different job

When I edit my team's work, I want it to sound like the best version of the writer in the brand's voice, not like me. That means explaining why a change helps instead of quietly making it. A good edit should make the next draft better too.

I also separate big notes from small ones. If a piece has a structural problem, line edits are a waste of everyone's time. I flag the structure first, let the writer fix it and save the sentence-level notes for the next round.

## Build your own checklist

Every writer has habits. Some overuse a favorite word. Others bury every conclusion or lean on the same sentence structure. The best editors know their own patterns and check for them on purpose.

I keep a short personal list of the things I catch myself doing, and I run every important draft against it before anyone else sees it. I encourage my team to do the same. A personal checklist turns feedback into a habit, and it means editors can spend their time on bigger questions instead of the same small fixes.

My list changes over time. When I stop making one mistake, another usually shows up to replace it. Updating the list keeps it honest and keeps me paying attention to my own work as closely as I pay attention to anyone else's.

## The short version

Edit in passes, and give the draft some distance. Fix structure before sentences, hunt for the buried lede and read it out loud. Check every fact. And when you're editing someone else, leave them better at it than they were.
