Two kinds of long-form content don't work. The first is written for search engines, stuffed with keywords and somehow says nothing in 3,000 words. The second reads like a sales brochure and never gets found.

At Nielsen, the goal was both. Our guides, reports and articles drove 30% organic traffic growth year over year, and they had to support sales too. Here's how I approach it.

## Start with the reader's question

Keyword research tells you what people type. It doesn't tell you why. Before I outline anything, I want to know what the reader is trying to get done and what they'd need to believe to take the next step.

If I can't answer that, the piece will rank for a while and convert nobody.

I also talk to the people who hear those questions every day. Sales and customer teams know which topics come up on calls, which objections stall deals and which misconceptions keep showing up. Their answers often point to better topics than any keyword tool.

![A line drawing of a magnifying glass over a page of text, with a question mark in the lens.](/img/search.svg)

## Earn the length

Long-form isn't a word count. It's a promise that the depth is worth it. Every section should answer something the reader actually wonders about. If a section exists only because the top-ranking competitor has one, I cut it or make it better.

The test I use: If a smart reader skimmed only the headings, would they learn something? If not, the structure isn't doing its job.

Length also has to be easy to move through. I front-load the most useful answer, keep paragraphs short and use lists, tables and examples where they help. A 2,500-word guide should feel shorter than it is.

> “Long-form isn't a word count. *It's a promise that the depth is worth it.*”

## Put the expertise up front

Readers and search engines are getting better at telling the difference between someone who knows a subject and someone who summarized five other articles. Original data, real examples and a clear point of view separate the two.

Nielsen had data nobody else had. Leading with it made our content more useful and more defensible at the same time. A competitor could copy our structure. They couldn't copy our numbers.

Most companies have more of this than they realize: internal benchmarks, customer patterns, lessons from hundreds of projects. Part of my job is finding that material and turning it into something a reader can use.

## Write it like a person

Long-form content gets stiff fast. Writers start hedging, stacking qualifiers and reaching for formal phrasing because the piece feels important. Readers notice, and they leave.

I edit long pieces with the same standards as a homepage: plain words, active voice and a clear point in every paragraph. Expertise reads best when it sounds like a smart colleague explaining something over coffee.

## Give readers somewhere to go

A guide that ranks and then leaves the reader with nowhere to go is a missed sale. I plan the next step before I write the first one:

- A related resource that goes one level deeper
- A natural point where talking to sales makes sense
- Content sales can send after a call, so the piece works twice

That last one matters more than people expect. When sales teams have a strong guide to share, they use it, and the content keeps working long after it's published.

## Measure past the click

Traffic is the easy number. I want to know whether readers stayed, moved deeper and eventually showed up in a sales conversation. That tells you whether long-form content is earning its budget.

I also revisit top pieces on a schedule. Data changes, competitors publish and search results shift. Refreshing a strong piece is often worth more than writing a new one.

## Plan content in clusters

One great guide can rank. A connected set of guides can own a topic. I plan long-form content in clusters: a core piece that covers the subject broadly, supported by deeper articles on specific questions, all linking to one another.

Clusters help readers move naturally from a basic question to a more advanced one. They also show search engines and AI tools that you cover a subject in depth, which makes each individual piece stronger.

Planning this way also keeps the editorial calendar focused. Instead of chasing random topics, the team builds steadily toward a few areas where the company wants to be known. Over time, that focus compounds, and the cluster becomes harder for competitors to match.

## The short version

Write for the reader's real question, and earn every section. Lead with what only you know, keep the writing human and always give readers somewhere to go next. Then keep the best pieces fresh, because long-form content is an asset that keeps paying off when you maintain it.
